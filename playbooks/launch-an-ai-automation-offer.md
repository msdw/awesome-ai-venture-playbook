# Launch an AI Automation Offer

## Goal

Package and sell an AI-powered workflow automation as a productized service that solves a specific, recurring business problem for a defined buyer.

## Who this is for

Consultants, freelancers, and technical builders who want to turn AI workflow automation skills into a repeatable offer they can sell to businesses. This playbook assumes you can build automations using tools such as n8n, Make, Zapier, LangChain, or custom Python scripts.

## Step-by-step breakdown

### Step 1 — Choose a focused problem

A strong automation offer solves one specific, high-frequency problem for one target buyer. Examples:

- Classifying and routing support tickets for customer service teams
- Summarizing weekly reports for operations managers
- Enriching CRM contacts from public sources for sales teams
- Generating weekly content briefs for marketing teams

Avoid broad offers like "AI automation for your business." Specificity builds trust and simplifies sales.

Scoring questions for the problem:
- Does the buyer do this task at least weekly?
- Does it currently require significant manual effort?
- Can AI meaningfully reduce that effort?
- Would time savings directly translate to value for the buyer?

### Step 2 — Define the deliverable

Write down exactly what you will deliver, in plain language:

> We will connect [source system], run [AI process], and deliver [output] to [destination] every [frequency].

Example:

> We will connect Zendesk, classify each new support ticket using AI, assign it to the right team, and send a daily digest summary to the support manager every morning.

Define clearly:
- What data goes in
- What the AI does
- What output the buyer receives
- Where the output lands (Slack, email, Google Sheets, CRM)
- What is excluded from scope

### Step 3 — Scope a minimal version

Build the simplest version that delivers the core value. Do not add features before you have paying customers.

Minimal version typically includes:
- One data source connected
- One AI transformation step
- One output format
- Manual trigger or simple scheduled run

Avoid scope creep. Every feature you add before sale reduces your speed and increases your risk.

### Step 4 — Price it correctly

Price based on value delivered, not hours spent. Common pricing models for automation offers:

- **Setup fee**: one-time fee to build and deploy the automation (typical range: $500 to $5,000 depending on complexity)
- **Monthly retainer**: recurring fee for maintenance, monitoring, and updates
- **Per-run fee**: charged per execution, suitable for variable volume clients
- **Bundled package**: setup + 3 months maintenance for a single price

Avoid hourly pricing. It shifts the focus to effort rather than value.

Test pricing by asking: "What would this save you per month?" A buyer who saves 10 hours at $100/hour per week has $4,000/month of value to capture from.

### Step 5 — Write a one-page offer document

Create a simple document that a buyer can understand in 2 minutes:

- The specific problem it solves
- How the automation works (3-4 bullet points)
- What the buyer receives
- Integrations required
- Pricing
- Timeline to deliver
- What you need from the buyer to start

This document should not be a detailed technical spec. It should be a clear agreement that sets expectations.

### Step 6 — Find 3 pilot buyers

Do not launch publicly before you have built and tested the automation with at least one real client.

Sources for first buyers:
- Existing clients or former employers
- LinkedIn outreach targeting the specific role you serve
- Industry communities and Slack groups
- Warm introductions from your network

Offer a reduced rate or pilot pricing for the first 2-3 clients in exchange for a testimonial and feedback.

### Step 7 — Deliver, document, and iterate

During delivery:
- Document every step of the build
- Capture screenshots of the working automation
- Record a short video walkthrough for the client
- Collect feedback after 2-4 weeks of operation

After delivery:
- Write a brief case study: before state, automation delivered, outcome measured
- Use the case study in future sales conversations

### Step 8 — Productize further

Once you have delivered the automation to 2-3 clients:
- Standardize the technical implementation into a repeatable template
- Write a deployment checklist
- Define what data access and permissions are required from each client
- Set up monitoring to detect when automations break

This turns a custom project into a scalable offer.

## Success criteria

- One specific problem and buyer defined in writing
- One minimal automation built and tested
- Two paying clients or committed pilot agreements
- A case study or client testimonial collected
- Pricing and scope clearly documented

## Common mistakes

**Building before selling.** Spend more time talking to potential buyers than building. Build only after you have a committed buyer.

**Choosing a problem that is too broad.** "Automate marketing" is not an offer. "Generate and send weekly social media content from your blog posts" is an offer.

**Underpricing setup work.** Setup costs should reflect the value delivered, not just your time. If you price too low, buyers will not take the offer seriously.

**Skipping documentation.** If the automation breaks and you have no documentation, it becomes a custom support engagement. Treat every automation as a product.

**Ignoring data privacy.** Automations often touch sensitive data. Understand what data the automation processes and whether any data protection obligations apply.

**Over-engineering the first version.** A working automation that requires minor manual steps is better than a fully automated system that is weeks away from delivery.
