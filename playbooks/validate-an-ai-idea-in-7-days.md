# Validate an AI Idea in 7 Days

## Goal

Determine whether a specific AI idea solves a real problem for a real audience before committing significant time or money to building it.

## Who this is for

Indie builders, consultants, founders, and product teams who have an AI idea they want to test quickly without building a full product. This playbook is for people who want evidence before committing to development.

## Day-by-day breakdown

### Day 1 — Define the hypothesis

Write a single-sentence problem statement:

> [Target user] struggles with [specific painful problem] because [root cause], which costs them [time, money, or risk].

Write a single-sentence solution hypothesis:

> An AI tool that [specific action] would allow [target user] to [desired outcome] in [time or effort reduction].

Be specific. Vague hypotheses produce vague results. If you cannot write these two sentences, your idea is not ready to validate.

### Day 2 — Identify 10 potential users

List 10 real people or organizations who likely have this problem. Do not list hypothetical personas — list actual contacts, communities, or companies.

Sources:
- Your professional network
- LinkedIn searches
- Slack or Discord communities related to the target industry
- Reddit communities
- Twitter/X searches for complaints about the problem
- Product Hunt comments on related tools

Prioritize people who have publicly complained about the problem or who fit the target profile closely.

### Day 3 — Conduct 5 problem interviews

Contact 10 people and aim for 5 conversations. Keep interviews focused on the problem, not the solution. Ask:

1. How do you currently handle [problem area]?
2. How much time does it take?
3. What is the worst part about the current process?
4. What have you already tried to improve this?
5. What would it be worth to you if this were solved well?

Do not show or pitch your solution during the interview. Listen.

### Day 4 — Analyze interview findings

After 5 conversations, answer:

- Did at least 3 people describe the same core problem unprompted?
- Did anyone use the words "frustrating", "annoying", "waste", "broken", or "we really need"?
- Did anyone describe a workaround they built themselves?
- Did anyone give a specific dollar amount or time cost?

If yes to 3 or more: the problem likely exists. Proceed.
If no: revise the hypothesis or consider a different problem.

### Day 5 — Build a minimal demo

Build the simplest possible demonstration:

- A prompt in ChatGPT with sample input and output
- A Google Sheets macro with AI integration
- A Streamlit app with one function
- A pre-recorded walkthrough of the concept with mock data

The demo does not need to work at scale. It needs to show the core value in 60 seconds.

### Day 6 — Get feedback on the solution

Show the demo to 3-5 people from your interview list. Ask:

1. Does this address the problem you described?
2. What would you change or add?
3. Would you use something like this? Under what conditions?
4. What would you expect to pay for this?

Document reactions. Look for "I would pay for this immediately" or "This is exactly what we need."

### Day 7 — Make a go/no-go decision

Summarize your findings:

- Problem evidence: how many people confirmed the problem?
- Solution fit: how many people responded positively to the demo?
- Willingness to pay: did anyone give a realistic price expectation?
- Risk signals: are there compliance, legal, or technical blockers?

Go if: problem is confirmed, solution fit is positive, willingness to pay exists, and risks are manageable.

No-go if: problem was weak, demo generated polite but not enthusiastic responses, or no willingness to pay surfaced.

## Success criteria

- At least 5 problem interviews completed
- At least 3 people confirmed the problem independently
- At least 1 person expressed clear interest in the demo
- A go/no-go decision is documented with evidence

## Common mistakes

**Skipping the interviews.** Building before validating is the most common mistake. The interviews are the validation.

**Pitching instead of listening.** During problem interviews, if you are describing your solution, you are not learning about the problem.

**Validating with friends and family.** People who want to be supportive will not give you honest signal. Speak to strangers who fit the target profile.

**Moving the goalposts.** If your interviews reveal the problem is different than you assumed, update the hypothesis before building — do not force-fit the solution.

**Treating one enthusiastic response as validation.** Look for patterns across 5+ conversations, not exceptions.

**Ignoring compliance early.** If the use case involves healthcare, legal, finance, or HR, assess regulatory requirements before building anything.
